## 2. Mechanism inventory

Rating = trust/conversion value. "Decorative" means it looks credible but has no evidence or mechanism for moving a purchase decision.

### High value

**Material provenance, named and specific**
*Who:* The Citizenry, Gaatha, Kazi. *How:* "elephant grass native to their area", named fibre, dye, source region.
*Why high:* **[Evidence]** Baymard's DTC study names *Materials & Ingredients* as one of five story types that users actively seek, and observed users **dismissing products that lacked detailed material descriptions**. This is not sentiment — it is spec data that happens to be a story.

**Process storytelling with numbers and time**
*Who:* The Citizenry ("split the elephant grass strands in two; twisting it together to make a stronger straw"; an 8-step process, up to a day per basket).
*Why high:* **[Evidence]** Baymard's fourth and fifth story types — *Manufacturing & Production Practices* and *Overall Product Quality* — were both cited by test users as purchase differentiators, with quality content specifically framed as justifying DTC premium pricing. **[Strong inference]** Labour-time is the single most legible price justification for handcraft. "One day of skilled work" explains a price in a way "artisan-made" never does.

**Story content surfaced early, not buried**
*Why high:* **[Evidence]** Baymard: **67% of users on DTC sites sought nonproduct content** vs 37% on traditional retail sites; **62% of test users engaged with About Us / Our Story pages**. The named failure mode was content that was *"too hidden"* — one participant: *"Why do they not have this on the homepage? Why so hidden?… I would have been hooked much sooner."*

**Reviews and community Q&A**
*Who:* weakest area across nearly every craft/impact site examined.
*Why high:* **[Evidence]** Baymard: **70% of sites lack the FAQ + community-Q&A combination**; **55% have no community Q&A at all**; **40% of test subjects used Q&A even when reviews were present**, because seller-authored claims carry perceived commercial bias. **[Evidence]** NN/g: in 20 sessions, **reviews were mentioned over 40 times**; users cross-reference third-party sources rather than trusting corporate messaging. Baymard's sustainability study additionally found users' unanswered sustainability questions cluster on *materials, reuse, product origin, certifications* — exactly MUD's territory.

**Authentic, uncaptioned-free photography**
*Why high:* **[Evidence]** NN/g lists stock photography among the top credibility destroyers and authentic imagery/video among the top credibility builders.

**Third-party certification — *with an explainer***
*Who:* The Citizenry (WFTO-audited, GOTS, OEKO-TEX, FSC), All Across Africa (Nest Seal), Indian equivalents (GI tag, Handloom Mark).
*Why high, conditionally:* **[Evidence]** Baymard found **37% of sites use industry jargon in filters and labels**, and unexplained sustainability terms go unused — one participant: *"What's defined as 'sustainable' or not? I don't know what a lot of these mean, so they're not that useful to me."* A badge without a one-line definition is decorative; a badge with a tooltip and a link to what was audited is a trust signal.

### Medium value

**Group / workshop profile as the storytelling unit**
*Who:* The Citizenry (workshop pages: group size, head-of-workshop quote, materials, process, gallery), Ten Thousand Villages, MADE51, Kazi.
*Why medium:* Structurally strong and maintainable, but on every site examined the partner page was a **terminal node** — The Citizenry's Bolgatanga page carries no product links at all. The story is read and the session ends.

**Individual named maker profiles**
*Who:* Etsy (seller-owned), Novica, Anchal.
*Why medium:* **[Evidence, vendor-published]** Etsy's Seller Handbook states **57% of buyers are willing to pay more for an item when they know who made it** — treat as a directional first-party claim, not independent research. **[Evidence]** Anchal's artisan page shows the failure case: first name, headshot, team role, a promise to *"discover the names and stories of these remarkable women below"* — and no stories, no bios, no product links, no metrics. Individual profiles are only high-value when the individual is actually attached to inventory.

**Quantified impact ledger**
*Who:* The Citizenry ("3,938 artisan partners", "23 countries", "2,384 female entrepreneurs", "$40.3 million invested", "on average 2x the fair trade wage requirement").
*Why medium:* **[Evidence]** Baymard's *Brand Values & Mission* story type showed users responding to corporate-responsibility content (*"I can see that when I purchase, I help this company make a difference"*). **[Strong inference]** Specific auditable numbers work; round adjectival claims do not, and NN/g's finding that users cross-check third-party sources means an unverifiable number is a liability.

**Wage-methodology disclosure**
*Who:* All Across Africa — fair wage set against three named inputs (value of time and materials, opportunity cost, cost of living), with artisan representatives at the negotiation.
*Why medium-to-high:* **[Strong inference]** This is the only mechanism found that answers "how do you *know* it's fair" rather than asserting it. Low salience for casual buyers, decisive for skeptical, press, wholesale and institutional buyers.

**Craft/technique and region landing pages**
*Who:* Gaatha (research + film + essay), iTokri (cluster-led), The Citizenry (country-grouped index).
*Why medium:* Strong for organic search and for browsing without a product in mind; weak as a direct conversion surface unless products are embedded in the page.

**Video / behind-the-scenes**
*Who:* Etsy shop video, Gaatha's *Hands of Handmade* film series.
*Why medium:* **[Evidence, vendor-published]** Etsy's buyer research is cited as showing video increases transparency and buyer confidence. Production cost is real; value is highest for process, lowest for talking-head narrative.

**Founder / origin story**
*Why medium:* **[Evidence]** Baymard's first story type; users sought founder information, valuing it as differentiation from *"soulless"* larger competitors. For MUD the founder is not the point — but the *why this enterprise exists* content occupies the same slot.

### Decorative (no evidence, no mechanism)

- **GPS coordinates as ornament.** The Citizenry stamps "10° 47' 02'' N 00° 51' 14'' W" on partner pages. Beautiful, unverifiable by the reader, changes nothing.
- **Full-bleed atmospheric portraits without names or captions.** Reads as aesthetic sourcing, not provenance — and edges toward the extractive framing MUD needs to avoid.
- **Maker name on category-grid tiles.** **[Evidence]** Baymard's product-listing research identifies five universal list-item attributes — price, title/type, thumbnail, ratings average *plus* count, and variations — with 1–3 category-specific specs. Maker name is not among them; **50% of sites already get list-item information wrong**. Adding maker names to tiles lowers signal-to-noise.
- **A standalone "Impact" page with no product path.** Present on most sites examined; a reading destination, not a buying one.
- **"Every purchase changes a life" copy without a number.** Assertion without verification, which NN/g's research says users route around.
- **Story-first site architecture that separates commerce from narrative.** Gaatha splits research (gaatha.org), storytelling (gaatha.com) and shop (shop.gaatha.com). Culturally excellent, commercially leaky — three domains means three funnels.
- **Hardship-first / beneficiary framing.** **[Evidence, moderate confidence]** The "poverty porn" literature (Mahmoud, *Journal of Philanthropy and Marketing*, 2024; Shabbir et al., *Business & Society*, 2025) documents that deficit framing may raise short-term funds while eroding trust and stripping subject agency. One secondary report of a 2024 Duncan/Levine/Small study claims viewers worry more about *being misled* than about emotional intensity — primary source unverified, treat as indicative only. Notably, The Citizenry's About page contains **no charity language at all**: partnership, entrepreneurship grants, and leadership recognition, never beneficiaries.

## 3. Product-page attribution patterns

Three archetypes, in descending strength:

**A. Marketplace attribution (Etsy, Novica).** The maker is the selling entity. Every listing carries a persistent, linked shop/artist identity; the About section is a structured record (story, shop members, production partners, photos/video), not free prose. Strongest possible attribution — but it requires per-maker inventory ownership, which a curated social enterprise usually does not have.

**B. Curated-brand attribution (The Citizenry, Kazi, Ten Thousand Villages).** The brand owns the SKU; the maker group is credited as "partner". Craft, material and process descriptions appear *inline on the PDP* ("handwoven by a cooperative of artisans in the remote region of Bolgatanga… an 8-step process that takes up to a day"), with a separate partner page as a destination. **The consistent defect: the link is one-directional.** The partner page has no route back to that group's products.

**C. Directory-only (MADE51, Anchal).** Profiles exist as a browsable list — MADE51 shows partner name, location, artisans' country of origin, craft description, with no per-partner pages and products routed through generic shop nav. Story and commerce never touch.

**[Evidence]** Baymard's sustainability study found **25% of sites fail to highlight core brand and product attributes on the product page** — the single most common structural miss, and exactly what archetypes B and C leak.

Two supporting patterns worth noting: **organisation-level rather than product-level certification** (WFTO issues a Product Label and a First Buyer Label to *member organisations* — meaning MUD would be claiming an audited organisation, not an audited item, and should say so precisely); and the near-universal **handmade variance disclaimer**, present across dozens of small craft sellers, framing colour/texture/size variation as characteristic rather than defect. No measured effect on returns or conversion was found for the latter.

## 4. Recommendations for MUD

1. **Make the community — not the individual — the primary storytelling entity.** *Why:* it matches the production reality of basketry, it is maintainable at scale (Anchal's 180-headshot page is already unmaintained), and it avoids singling out individuals for a Western gaze. Name individuals *within* the community profile, with consent, as voices — the Citizenry's head-of-workshop quote pattern.

2. **Structure the community profile as a schema, not an essay.** Fields: community/cluster name · district + state · craft tradition · materials and where they are gathered · process steps with time-per-unit · number of makers · working since · one named voice + quote · unstyled photo set · what MUD pays and how it's set. *Why:* **[Evidence]** all five Baymard story types map to fields, not paragraphs; schema also makes the content comparable, filterable and translatable.

3. **Make attribution bidirectional and mandatory.** PDP → community page → **that community's products**. *Why:* this is the defect in every archetype-B site examined; fixing it is close to free and converts story readers into browsers.

4. **Put a "How this was made" block inside the buy column**, not below the fold: material, process step count, hours per piece, community name, one link. *Why:* **[Evidence]** Baymard's 25%-fail finding on product-page attributes, plus the *"too hidden"* failure mode.

5. **One badge row, every badge with a tooltip and a verification link.** *Why:* **[Evidence]** Baymard's 37% jargon finding. Uninterpreted marks are ignored; explained marks are used.

6. **Replace impact adjectives with three auditable numbers plus a methodology page.** Model the wage disclosure on All Across Africa: state the inputs to the wage calculation and who was in the room. *Why:* **[Evidence]** NN/g — users cross-reference; unverifiable claims are the thing they are checking *for*.

7. **Ship reviews and a community Q&A before shipping more story.** *Why:* **[Evidence]** the largest documented gap in the category (70%/55%/40% Baymard findings) and the highest-credibility content type available, because it is not authored by the seller. Seed it with real questions about variation, care and durability.

8. **Treat handmade variance as a published spec, not a legal disclaimer.** State the expected variation range and show two units side by side. *Why:* **[Strong inference]** it converts the top return-risk of handcraft into direct evidence of the handmade claim.

9. **Photograph work, not poverty.** Hands, tools, materials, workshop; caption every person shown with name and role, or don't show a face. No stock imagery anywhere. *Why:* **[Evidence]** NN/g credibility builders/destroyers.

10. **Story lives in main navigation; commerce is never gated behind it.** *Why:* **[Evidence]** 62% of users go looking for it — so it must be findable — but Gaatha's three-domain split shows what happens when the narrative becomes a toll gate.

11. **Explicitly ban charity register from the copy deck.** Partner, community, craft, wage, order. Not beneficiary, help, rescue, give back.

## 5. Open questions

- **No published A/B test of maker attribution on conversion was found** for craft ecommerce. Every conversion number surfaced in search was SEO-blog restatement without a primary source (excluded). The Etsy 57% figure is first-party marketing content. This is a genuine evidence gap, not a search failure.
- **Individual vs collective attribution:** no comparative study exists. Does naming one weaver outperform naming a 40-person cooperative, or does it read as tokenising? Testable by MUD.
- **Domestic vs export audience:** does GI-tag / cluster naming carry more weight with domestic buyers than a personal maker narrative, and does the reverse hold for export? Untested in anything found.
- **Do impact ledgers get read?** No eye-tracking or scroll-depth data located for impact modules on commerce pages. They may be entirely below the abandonment point.
- **Variance disclosure:** does it reduce returns, suppress conversion, or both? The pattern is universal and the evidence is zero.
- **Physical provenance tags:** a QR-traceability willingness-to-pay study (median ~$1.45 marginal WTP, +$0.33–0.38 when blockchain-verified) exists for *leafy greens*. Whether that transfers to craft is unknown and the effect size is small enough that the tag cost may exceed it.
- **Consent and privacy model:** none of the sites examined disclose how maker names, photographs and quotes are obtained, compensated or revocable. This is an ethics requirement for MUD's brief, and also an unexploited trust signal — nobody in the category publishes their consent policy.
