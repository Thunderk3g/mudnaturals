What we learned — in one line each

The 7 findings that matter most. Everything else is in the research folder if you want depth.

🕳️

Nobody in Nepal does what we plan.

No shop combines beautiful curated homeware + easy Nepali payment + showing who made each product. The spot is empty.

💵

People pay more when they see the maker.

Research shows shoppers pay roughly 17% more for products where the making is visible. That is our whole idea.

📦

8 of 10 online orders in Nepal are Cash on Delivery.

We must offer it — but with a phone check first, or a quarter of parcels come back unpaid.

🏷️

Show the full price early.

The #1 reason shoppers quit is surprise delivery costs at the end. Price + delivery shown up front, always.

🔁

eSewa won't tell us when someone pays.

Their system only redirects the shopper's browser. We must double-check every payment ourselves. Solved — but it shapes the build.

🧰

The old ecommerce code is a parts bin, not a foundation.

It has serious hidden bugs. We keep 4 good parts and build the rest fresh, smaller and safer.

🤝

Talk craft, not charity.

"Woven over two days from sikki grass in Rautahat" sells. "Help poor artisans" doesn't — and can even harm trust.

The market, simply

Three kinds of players exist today. Each has one strength and one weakness.

🏛️ The fair-trade veterans

Mahaguthi · Sana Hastakala · Dhukuti · WSDO

Strong: 40 years of real artisan relationships and real impact.
Weak: websites feel like 2014; huge unedited catalogues; some don't even show prices.

✨ The young design brands

Kolpa · Mheecha · Aji's · Timro · Local Project

Strong: modern taste; proof Nepalis pay Rs 2,000–4,000 for good local products.
Weak: tiny teams, mostly physical shops, weak or no online checkout.

🛒 The marketplaces

Daraz · Saino · Swodeshi · Himali Green

Strong: apps, payments, delivery all work.
Weak: everything is for sale, so nothing feels special. No curation, no story.

The gap MUD fills

A beautifully curated shop + easy Nepali payment (eSewa / Khalti / COD) + every product showing who made it and where. Each piece is proven to work in Nepal — no one has put them together.

Question 1 · Store type

Should MUD be a curated shop — we choose every product — rather than an open marketplace where anyone can sell? Research says yes

Marketplaces already exist in Nepal (Daraz, Saino) and earn tiny commissions. A curated shop keeps quality, story and margin in our hands.

Yes → We build one branded shop. Makers stay behind the scenes as partners; we can still add more makers over time.
No → We build seller sign-up, seller tools and dispute handling — a much bigger, thinner-margin build competing head-on with Daraz and Saino.
Later → Design can't start on the shop structure until this is decided. It is the biggest fork in the road.
Question 2 · Our promise

Should our promise to customers be: "every object shows who made it and where it came from"? Research says yes

Everyone in Nepal claims to support artisans. Nobody actually shows the maker on the product page. This scored highest of 6 positioning options we tested.

Yes → Every product page gets a "who made this" section, and we collect maker info for every item before it goes live.
No → We fall back to "beautiful Nepali design" — nice, but two brands already claim it and it's easy to copy.
Later → The website's signature feature stays undesigned.
Question 3 · Paying makers

Will MUD buy products from makers up front (we own the stock), rather than paying them only after items sell?

Buying up front is simpler and fairer to makers (they get paid immediately), but ties up our cash. Pay-after-sale needs a running money ledger per maker.

Yes → Simple stock and payments system. We carry the inventory risk.
No → We build a per-maker earnings ledger and payout process into the admin system.
Later → The database design for stock and payments waits on this.
Question 4 · Naming makers

Can we show makers' real names and photos on the website — with their written permission?

Named real people are our strongest trust signal. But it needs consent from each maker, and some may prefer their group/community named instead.

Yes → We create simple consent forms and build maker profile pages with names, photos and quotes.
No → We name the community/village instead of the person — still works, slightly weaker emotionally.
Later → Ask the makers first — this is really their decision. Design will prepare both versions.
Question 5 · Cash on Delivery

Should we offer Cash on Delivery at launch — with a phone confirmation before we ship? Research says yes

~80% of Nepali online orders are COD. Refusing it kills sales. But unconfirmed COD means ~1 in 4 parcels comes back — the phone check fixes most of that.

Yes → Checkout gets a COD option + an OTP/WhatsApp confirmation step before dispatch.
No → eSewa-only checkout. Simpler build, but expect far fewer first-time buyers.
Later → Checkout design proceeds with COD included; easy to switch off.
Question 6 · Gifting

Should we sell corporate & festival gift boxes from day one?

Banks and IT firms already buy Dashain/Tihar gifts every year — mostly chocolate and dry fruit. Craft-grade gifting is an open lane, and bulk orders give us steady income.

Yes → A "Gifting" page + a bulk-enquiry form at launch, and a gift-box product line in the first collection.
No → Consumer shop only; gifting added later once operations settle.
Later → We keep a simple enquiry form either way — costs almost nothing.
Question 7 · Stories

Will someone on the team publish one story/article per month (a maker visit, a craft, a material)?

Stories bring free Google traffic and make the brand feel alive. But a journal with 3 old posts looks worse than no journal.

Yes → "Journal" goes in the main menu; we launch with 8–10 stories ready.
No → No journal in the menu. Maker pages alone carry the story — still strong.
Later → Design prepares the journal but keeps it out of the menu until you commit.
Question 8 · Photography

Is there budget for one professional photo shoot (products + makers at work + villages)?

Photography is the single biggest thing that makes a shop feel premium. Every premium brand we studied is carried by its photos.

Yes → We design a photo-led website and write a shot list for the photographer.
No → We switch to a typography-led design that still looks premium with modest photos. It works — different flavour.
Later → Get one quote from a Kathmandu studio first; the design direction genuinely depends on this.
Question 9 · Where we ship

Ship only within Nepal at launch? Research says yes

International cards + air freight from Nepal are genuinely hard. The smart bridge: let Nepalis abroad order gifts delivered to family in Nepal — no export needed.

Yes → Nepal-only delivery + a "send a gift home" flow for the diaspora. Export becomes phase 2.
No → We must solve international payments and expensive shipping before launch — expect months of delay.
Later → Build proceeds Nepal-only; nothing is lost by deciding later.
Question 10 · Language

English-only website at launch? Research says yes

Our first buyers (urban Kathmandu + diaspora) shop comfortably in English. A half-translated site feels less trustworthy than a confident English one.

Yes → English now, built so Nepali can be added later without rework.
No → We add Nepali translation work to the launch plan — more time, needs a translator.
Later → Build proceeds in English with translation kept possible.
